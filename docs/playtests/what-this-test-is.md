# What the ten-minute run is, and what this test is actually asking

**Who this is for:** anyone. You do not need to know the game, the code, or anything
about Congress. Five minutes to read.

---

## Part 1 — What you would see if you sat down right now

A beige desk with **twelve cards** on it, laid out in three rows. Above them, a strip
showing what week it is, which party you belong to, and six numbers about how your
office is doing. On the right, a panel telling you who you are: a first-term member of
the House, elected as a Democrat, who promised two things and now has to build a
housing bill.

Someone drags one card onto another. A **Policy Aide** onto a **Rent Burden Report**.

The two cards snap together. A green bar appears along the bottom. They press **Resume**,
the bar fills over six seconds, and the two cards vanish — replaced by a new one:
**Evidence Summary**. A line of text says what just happened.

Then they hand you the mouse and stop talking.

### What you have to work out on your own

Nobody tells you the rules. There are three of them, and one of them can change.

**Rule one.** You just watched it. But look at the desk: there is a second report called
**Tenant Survey**, and a second Policy Aide. Does the rule need *that exact report*, or
does it work with any housing evidence?

It works with either. That is the first thing the game wants you to notice — and it is
the whole design in miniature.

**Rule two.** You now hold an Evidence Summary. Put it with the **Housing Choice
Voucher** and you get a **Drafted Provision** — the card says *"Bill language."* You just
turned research into something a bill could contain. In this ten-minute version that is
the end of that thread; the finished game adds a step for putting it into your bill, and
the card says so if you hover it.

**Rule three.** Take your **Working Bill** to another member's office and ask for support.
There are two offices on the desk. **Hillcrest** works. **Ridgeline** bounces off and
nothing happens.

Why? Look at the cards. Hillcrest has a **D**. Ridgeline has an **R**. You are a Democrat.
The rule only accepts offices from your own party.

### The bit the whole test is really about

There is one card left over: **Bipartisan Working Group**.

You cannot press a button to use it. You drag a Policy Aide onto it and wait eight
seconds while your staffer studies it.

When they finish, rule three has changed. It now accepts the other party too. Go back to
Ridgeline and it works — they will help, if you take their amendment, which costs you a
little of what you promised.

That is the run. Three rules found, one rule widened, roughly twenty-six seconds of
actual waiting. The rest of the ten minutes is you trying things.

**Nothing you try can hurt you.** Cards that do not go together simply bounce apart. No
cost, no penalty, no time lost. Experimenting is free on purpose.

## Part 2 — The one bet the whole game rests on

Here is the thing worth being clear about, because everything else follows from it.

**A phrasebook gets you through a menu. Grammar lets you say things nobody taught you.**

A card game can teach you either.

If Paper Majority teaches a **phrasebook**, then "Policy Aide plus Rent Burden Report
makes an Evidence Summary" is a fact you memorise. Add the other 69 cards and you have
roughly two hundred facts to memorise. That is not a game, that is homework.

If it teaches **grammar**, then what you learned is: *a policy-focused staffer can
summarise any housing evidence.* Now you can look at a card you have never seen, read
what kind of thing it is, and work out what it will do. Two hundred combinations become
a handful of rules you can reason with.

Everything in the design serves the second one. The cards deliberately come in matched
pairs — two reports that both work, two offices that behave differently — so the game is
constantly asking *"is it the specific card, or the kind of card?"*

And the Bipartisan Working Group is grammar changing shape. Not a key that opens one
locked door. A rule that now accepts a wider class of things, everywhere, for the rest
of the game.

**The test asks one question: does the grammar land, and is using it enjoyable?**

---

## Part 3 — What this test is for

Two things, and they are equally important.

**Can a stranger work out the rules by trying things?** Measured by whether they can
predict combinations they were never shown, whether they find rules without opening the
help, and whether they can explain what changed when the rule widened.

**Does doing that feel good?** Measured by whether they choose to play again, and by
whether setbacks feel like their own doing rather than something the game did to them.

Six specific things get scored. They are listed in the facilitator's guide. But those six
are just the operational version of those two questions.

There is also one thing with no score attached, which is often the most useful moment in
the session: **watch for when they stop guessing.** It looks like a pause, then a
deliberate reach for one particular card. That is the grammar landing. Write down when it
happened, or that it never did.

---

## Part 4 — What this test is NOT

This matters as much as what it is. Every one of these has a reason.

**It is not a demo.** You are not showing the game off. You are watching someone struggle
and writing down where. A session where the tester is confused is a *good* session — it
found something.

**It is not a test of the whole game.** The finished game runs 24 weeks and 45–60
minutes, with elections, committee votes, news events, and about 77 cards. Almost none of
that exists. Judging the game from this is like judging a novel by one paragraph.

**It is not a test of the artwork.** The cards are flat rectangles with emoji. Real
illustrations are being deliberately withheld until this passes, because art is the most
expensive thing to redo. If someone says it looks unfinished, they are correct and it
does not count against anything.

**It is not a test of anyone's knowledge of Congress.** If a tester does not know what a
"provision" is, that is a fact about the *game's* wording, not about the tester. The
design promises an interface readable "without requiring a civics textbook." Confusion
about a word is a bug report about the word.

**It is not a test of the person.** Say this out loud to them before you start. People
who think they are being examined stop trying things, and trying things is the entire
measurement.

**It is not a bug hunt.** Bugs get written down when they appear, but nobody is combing
for them. If the game breaks, note it, mark that session as spoiled, and move on.

**It is not a survey of opinions.** "Did you like it?" is not one of the six measures.
"Would you play again?" is — because that is behaviour, and behaviour is data. Opinions
are cheap; what someone actually chooses to do is not.

**It is not a vote on whether the game should exist.** It is a gate on one narrow
question. A failure does not mean "this is a bad idea." It means the words, the hints or
the feedback need work before anyone spends weeks building thirty more cards on top.

---

## Part 5 — What happens with the answer

| Result | What follows |
|---|---|
| All six measures clear their bar | Build the next 30 cards, real member offices from public records, sixteen rules, the events system |
| Any measure misses | Change the wording, hints and feedback. Run this test again. Build nothing new until it passes. |

---

## Part 6 — Why do this now, and not later

Because the mistakes get more expensive every week.

Right now the game has 8 kinds of card and 3 rules. If people cannot read them, the fix
is rewriting some words and adjusting some hints — a day or two.

The next stage is 30 cards, 16 rules, a dozen event scripts, and eight finished
illustrations. If the same problem is still there, the fix is all of that work done twice.

The stage after that is 77 cards and a full art library.

So the whole point of stopping here, with something deliberately unfinished and slightly
embarrassing to show people, is that **this is the cheapest moment it will ever be to
find out we are wrong.**

That is also why the run is ten minutes and not an hour. Ten minutes is long enough to
learn three rules and watch one of them change. Anything longer is testing content we
have not earned the right to build yet.

---

## In one paragraph

Twelve cards, three rules, one of which widens when a staffer studies a method. Ten
minutes. We are finding out whether a stranger can work out the rules by trying things —
and whether that feels good enough to justify building the rest. It is not a demo, not a
test of the art, not a test of the person, and not a verdict on the game. It is the
cheapest possible way to find out we are wrong before being wrong gets expensive.
